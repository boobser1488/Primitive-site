Survival and the body

The body does not know itself in percentages, and neither does the game: it speaks in states.

Reading the body

Three bars sit in the lower right: water, stamina and food. The pack's BODY tab shows everything else as a bar and a word: health, food, water, stamina, tiredness, warmth, wetness, dirt, diet and recovery, with a list of wounds and what each needs. If you want numbers instead of words, turn on DETAILED NUMBERS in the settings.

Food

Work burns food, time barely does. Standing still you would take about forty minutes to empty the bar; digging, running and jumping are what empty it. An empty bar does not kill at once, and the order matters more than the numbers:

  1. Below about 70%, wounds stop closing. The bar darkens at that line.
  2. At zero you are weak but alive: blows are a quarter weaker, breath comes back a third slower, and in the cold you feel four degrees colder.
  3. Only after four game days without food does health start to go.

Foraging alone will not feed you. A berry bush is rare and takes three game days to bear again. Hunting, the fire, the drying rack and the field are what feed you.

Food spoils. Raw meat keeps a day, cooked meat two; then the stack turns rotten. Cold keeps it: at zero and below nothing ages, and between 0 and 12° things age half as fast. A cellar dug a few blocks into the ground keeps the year's average temperature. Dry food (grain, flour) never spoils. Stacks of different age sit in different slots.

Water

An empty water bar is three stages, counted in game days, not half a minute to death:

  1. Thirsty: breath comes back more slowly.
  2. Weak, after half a day without water: you walk slower, hit weaker, heal worse.
  3. Faint, after a day and a quarter: you fall, and only a drink gets you up.

Any drink ends all of it. Drink from running water; see Getting started for pond and sea water. A jug carries one measure of water and remembers what kind it is.

Swimming next update

A swimmer lies along the water, and only the chest and head near the surface touch the world. So you swim over an underwater ledge without catching your feet on it, and you can pass under water through a gap one block high (under a raft, into a flooded passage) by diving. Where the water over the bottom is shallower than your waist you stand up on your own; under a low roof, where there is nowhere to stand, you keep swimming. Looking steeply down is a dive: against a wall you slide down it instead of being lifted along it.

The stroke is slow, like a dolphin's: both arms work together, a long glide, a pull under the chest to the hips, and a wave runs through the body from head to feet. A stroke takes 1.8 seconds at full speed and four when you hold in place, and the splash sounds once a stroke, when the hands enter the water.

Stamina

Running, jumping, digging, hitting and throwing all cost stamina. A full bar is about 22 seconds of running or a dozen jumps; an empty one gives no jump at all. It comes back while you stand, slower when you carry a lot. Exhausted, you still dig, at half speed. Running is only forward or diagonally forward; backwards and sideways is a walk.

Cold, shelter and fire

Nights are cold, and the worst hour is the one before sunrise. On the starting meadow a night in the open without clothes brings shivering by morning and hunger twice as fast; in autumn and winter it crosses the line where health goes.

  • Open sky: the whole night is yours.
  • A roof: any opaque block over your head within 16 cells (leaves do not count). Takes away half the night's cold and all the rain.
  • A room: a roof plus walls at least two blocks high on three sides of four, within three cells. Takes away four-fifths of the night. A door can be the fourth side.
  • A fire inside the walls warms the whole room within five cells, in any season. The same fire in the open warms only a circle round itself.

Clothing, wool and rain

Clothes keep you warm in proportion to how dry they are: wet clothing stops warming. Rain soaks you slowly, and each kind of clothing sheds rain differently:

  • Wool is the warmest thing you can wear and lets almost all rain through. Dry, it is the answer to the north; soaked, it is nearly worthless. Crossing cold country in wool is a plan, not a stat.
  • Leather is less warm and keeps the rain off for a long time.
  • Fur (a hood and a cape sewn from fresh hides and sinew of the same animal, by hand, the day you kill it) is warmer than leather, colder than wool, and heavy.
  • Cloth (from cotton) is the only clothing that keeps you cooler than bare skin in the sun.

Wading wets only your legs; swimming soaks you through at once. You dry at a fire and in the sun. Things in the pack get wet too: wet sticks, fibre, logs and charcoal will not light, a wet torch will not light, wet flour goes mouldy. A minute by a fire dries the whole pack.

Heat

In the savanna and the desert the sun adds up to fourteen degrees at noon on top of the air. Standing in the open desert from sunrise, you are hot by ten and overheating by half past eleven, and from then on health goes. Any roof or tree crown removes the sun completely. Water cools three times faster than air. Travel in the morning and evening; at noon, stand under a tree.

To dress a wound, open the pack, take the bandage, splint or poultice and click the body part on the figure. Parts it can help light up green. Bandaged wounds heal gradually, and six times faster in sleep. A drop of blood next to the health bar means you are bleeding; a bone means something is broken.

Falls: three blocks costs nothing, six a scratch, twelve half your health, eighteen all of it. A full pack nearly doubles that. Water saves you only if it is deep: four blocks or more takes the whole fall.

What you wear counts. A rucksack (8 leather + 4 cord + 2 worked sticks by hand, or 6 leather at a leather bench) adds twenty slots; wear it in the back square. It cannot be taken off with things inside.

Downed, dead, and the way back

When health runs out you usually fall rather than die. The screen shows a countdown and what will get you up: a bandage for a wound, a splint for broken legs, warmth for cold, food for hunger, a drink for thirst. You can crawl, slowly. A friend can get you up by right-clicking you with the right thing. R gives up.

After death everything you carried stays in your body where you fell, including what you wore. The body keeps it all for two days (longer in the cold); then it is bones, and the soft half of its contents (food, leather, fur, wool, cloth, fibre, cord, hides, sinew) is gone. Metal, stone, flint, bone, fired clay and wood remain. If you carry a map, the places you died are marked on it.
